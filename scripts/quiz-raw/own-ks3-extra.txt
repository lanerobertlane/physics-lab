## Uncertainty & means
num | reference | Uncertainty & means | A student measures the same length three times: 12.4 cm, 12.6 cm and 12.5 cm. What is the mean? {both} | NUM: 12.5 cm | EXP: Mean = (12.4 + 12.6 + 12.5) ÷ 3 = 37.5 ÷ 3 = 12.5 cm.
num | reference | Uncertainty & means | Repeat readings are 2.1 Ω, 2.3 Ω and 2.2 Ω. What is the uncertainty, using half the range? {ks4} | NUM: 0.1 Ω | EXP: Range = 2.3 − 2.1 = 0.2 Ω. Uncertainty = ± 0.2 ÷ 2 = ± 0.1 Ω.
mc | reference | Uncertainty & means | Readings of 15.2 s, 15.4 s, 21.0 s and 15.3 s are taken. What should you do with 21.0 s? {both} | *Leave it out of the mean as an anomalous result; Include it in the mean; Change it to 15.3 s; Leave out all the readings | EXP: It doesn’t fit the pattern of the others, so it is anomalous. Leave it out of the mean, and repeat it if you can.
mc | reference | Uncertainty & means | What is the resolution of a ruler marked in millimetres? {both} | *1 mm; 1 cm; 0.1 mm; 10 cm | EXP: The resolution is the smallest division on the scale, which is 1 mm.
mc | reference | Uncertainty & means | Where should your eye be when you read a measuring cylinder? {both} | *Level with the bottom of the meniscus; Above the top of the liquid; Below the bottom of the cylinder; It doesn’t matter | EXP: Looking from above or below gives a parallax error. Read the bottom of the meniscus with your eye level with it.
mc | reference | Uncertainty & means | A balance reads 0.3 g with nothing on it. What kind of error is this? {ks4} | *A zero error, which is a systematic error; A random error; An anomalous result; A resolution error | EXP: Every reading will be 0.3 g too high, so the results are not accurate. Repeating doesn’t help: you subtract 0.3 g or zero the balance.
tf | reference | Uncertainty & means | Repeating readings and taking a mean reduces the effect of random errors. {both} | TRUE | EXP: Random errors make readings spread above and below the true value, so they partly cancel out in a mean.

## Practical graph plotter

mc | reference | Practical graph plotter | What does a straight line through the origin show? {both} | *The two quantities are directly proportional; There is no relationship; One quantity is constant; The results are anomalous | EXP: If one quantity doubles, the other doubles too.
tf | reference | Practical graph plotter | You should join the points on a graph dot to dot. {both} | FALSE | EXP: Draw a line of best fit: a straight line or smooth curve through the middle of the points.
num | reference | Practical graph plotter | A straight line of best fit goes up by 12 cm over 6 N. What is its gradient? {ks4} | NUM: 2 cm/N | EXP: Gradient = change in y ÷ change in x = 12 ÷ 6 = 2 cm/N.
mc | reference | Practical graph plotter | Why should you draw a large triangle to find the gradient of a line? {ks4} | *It makes the readings more accurate; It changes the gradient; It makes the line straighter; It removes anomalies | EXP: Bigger changes in x and y mean that small errors reading the graph matter less.
mc | reference | Practical graph plotter | When you choose the scales for a graph, how much of the grid should your points cover? {both} | *At least half of it, in each direction; About a quarter of it; Just the bottom row of squares; It doesn't matter | EXP: Choose scales so the points spread over at least half the grid each way. Then you can plot them and read the graph more accurately.
tf | reference | Practical graph plotter | The axes of a graph must always start at zero. {both} | FALSE | EXP: They don't have to. If starting at zero would squash your points into a small part of the grid, start the axis at a sensible value below your smallest reading.
mc | reference | Practical graph plotter | Which of these is the best set of steps for the scale on an axis? {both} | *0, 5, 10, 15, 20; 0, 3, 6, 9, 12; 0, 7, 14, 21, 28; 0, 6, 12, 18, 24 | EXP: Go up in steps of 1, 2 or 5 (or 10, 20, 50 and so on). Steps of 3, 6 or 7 make it hard to plot points between the lines.
