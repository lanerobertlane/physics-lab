Conductors & insulators

Which materials let a current flow? Drag a material into the gap in the circuit, watch the lamp and the ammeter, and look inside the material to see what its electrons are doing.

How it works

  • Metals are conductors. In a metal, some electrons aren’t held by any one atom. These free electrons can move through the whole metal. When the circuit is complete, the cell pushes them round it, and that flow of charge is the current.
  • Most non-metals are insulators. Plastic, wood, rubber and paper are made of non-metal elements. In them, and in glass, each electron stays with its own atom, so nothing can flow and there’s no current.
  • Graphite is the exception. It’s a form of carbon, a non-metal, but it has free electrons that can move along its layers. That’s why pencil lead lights the lamp, though it doesn’t conduct as well as a metal, so the lamp is dimmer.
  • Insulators keep us safe: the plastic around wires and plugs stops the current reaching you.
  • The blue dots show electrons, which flow from the negative terminal of the cell, round the circuit, to the positive terminal. Diagrams usually show the current the other way, from + to −. That’s called conventional current.

How to do it for real

  1. Connect a cell, a lamp and an ammeter in series, with a gap between two crocodile clips.
  2. Touch the clips together to check that the lamp lights.
  3. Clip each material into the gap. Watch the lamp and read the ammeter.
  4. Record whether each material is a metal, and whether it’s a conductor or an insulator.

Use a cell or a low-voltage power supply. Never test materials with mains electricity.
