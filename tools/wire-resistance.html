Resistance of a wire

Required practical: how does the length of a wire affect its resistance? Move the clip to change the length, read the two meters and use resistance = potential difference ÷ current.

Resistance against length

  • The line is straight, so every extra centimetre adds the same resistance. Resistance is directly proportional to length.
  • In a longer wire, the electrons have more ions to collide with on their way through, so it’s harder for the current to flow.
  • The line should go through the origin. If it doesn’t, that’s a zero error: the clips and their connections have a small resistance of their own, or the first clip isn’t exactly at 0 cm. It moves the whole line up, but the gradient stays the same.

This required practical also covers resistors in series and in parallel. You can build those in the Circuit builder.

How to do it for real

  1. Tape the wire along a metre ruler. Put one crocodile clip at 0 cm.
  2. Connect the cell, switch and ammeter in series with the wire, and the voltmeter in parallel with the length you’re testing.
  3. Put the other clip at a measured length, such as 10 cm.
  4. Close the switch, read both meters, then open the switch straight away.
  5. Work out the resistance: R = V ÷ I.
  6. Repeat for at least five lengths, then take a second set of readings and work out the means.
  7. Plot resistance against length, and draw a line of best fit.

Independent variable: the length. Dependent variable: the resistance. Control variables: the material and thickness of the wire, and its temperature. The current heats the wire and that raises its resistance, so use a low p.d. and open the switch between readings. A very short length with a high p.d. can get hot enough to burn you, or to melt.
