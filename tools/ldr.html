Light-dependent resistors (LDRs)

How does the light intensity on an LDR affect its resistance? Slide the LDR closer to the lamp or further away, read the two meters and use resistance = potential difference ÷ current.

Resistance against distance

  • Close to the lamp, a lot of light falls on the LDR: the light intensity is high, so its resistance is low.
  • Further away, the lamp’s light has spread out more, so less of it reaches the LDR. The light intensity is lower and the resistance is higher.
  • The graph is a curve, not a straight line, so the resistance isn’t directly proportional to the distance.
  • With the lamp off, the LDR is in the dark: its resistance is very high, so hardly any current flows.
  • Light from anywhere else, such as the room lights, would reach the LDR too and change its resistance. That’s why you do this in a darkened room.
  • LDRs are used in circuits that switch lights on automatically when it gets dark, such as street lights and night lights.

You can also put an LDR in a circuit of your own in the Circuit builder, and change the light level there.

How to do it for real

  1. Connect the LDR in series with a cell and an ammeter, and connect a voltmeter in parallel with the LDR.
  2. Put the lamp at the end of a ruler, with the LDR facing it along the ruler. Darken the room.
  3. Put the LDR at a measured distance from the lamp, such as 5 cm.
  4. Read both meters and work out the resistance: R = V ÷ I.
  5. Move the LDR to at least four more distances and repeat. Take a second set of readings and work out the means.
  6. Plot resistance against distance, and draw a smooth curve of best fit.

Independent variable: the distance from the lamp. Dependent variable: the resistance of the LDR. Control variables: the same lamp at the same brightness, the same LDR, and no other light in the room. The lamp gets hot, so don’t touch the bulb or its housing.
